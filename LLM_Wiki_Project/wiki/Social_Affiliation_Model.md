# Concept: Social Affiliation Model

## Definition
The Social Affiliation Model, originally proposed by O'Connor & Rosenblood (1996), is a psychological framework that describes the internal regulatory processes through which people monitor their need for affiliation with others. It posits that individuals possess an intrinsic need for social contact and will adjust their behaviors to maintain an optimal level of social interaction.

## Key Details
When social interactions fail to provide the expected feedback or fulfillment, individuals experience a state of social deprivation. According to findings by Tang et al. (2023) in the context of [[AI_and_Work]], interactions with non-human entities like artificial intelligence can trigger this deprivation. This state of unfulfilled social needs leads to dual coping mechanisms:
- **Adaptive Coping:** An increased need for affiliation, which can motivate positive, pro-social behaviors such as helping coworkers.
- **Maladaptive Coping:** Increased feelings of [[Workplace_Loneliness]] and withdrawal, potentially spilling over into negative after-work outcomes like insomnia or alcohol consumption.

These regulatory responses can be moderated by individual differences, such as a person's level of [[Attachment_Anxiety]].
